# Positioning Lab

## Positioning is a market choice

Positioning determines the situation in which a buyer should remember the offer, the alternative it replaces, the proof that makes the promise credible, and the operating choices required to deliver it.

“For designers,” “premium,” “friendly,” and “I help X achieve Y” may describe a niche or benefit. They do not by themselves make a same-product offer stand out.

## Generate materially different territories

Create at least three territories using different primary levers:

| Lever | Question |
|---|---|
| Career adjacency | Which buyer world, jargon, workflow, or relationships does the CV make unusually accessible? |
| Situation/occasion | At what moment, place, trigger, or ritual does the product become especially relevant? |
| Form factor/packaging | Can the physical or digital form remove embarrassment, friction, storage, setup, or decision effort? |
| Risk removal/service | Can fit, returns, onboarding, speed, verification, curation, or support remove the buyer's main fear? |
| Distribution/availability | Can the offer win by being present where/when others are inconvenient? |
| Identity/community | Which identity or affiliation does ownership signal, and is that identity evidenced rather than invented? |
| Category/reframe | Which alternative budget or behavior should the buyer compare against? |
| Bundle/value ladder | Can the same core item become a starter, kit, subscription, program, or system? |
| Partner/second market | Is another actor better positioned to distribute, recommend, implement, or resell it? |
| Brand architecture | Would a focused child brand open a market the parent cannot credibly enter, and can the team fund both? |

Narrowing an audience is one lever, not the entire exercise.

**Lever uniqueness rule.** Name the primary lever for each territory. Two territories may not
share a primary lever — three service packages built on career adjacency are one territory
described three ways. In recorded sessions, models repeatedly produced "three territories"
that were adjacent versions of the same offer, and each then chose between them as if the
comparison meant something.

**The renamed-job test.** Before presenting a territory, ask: strip the name away — what does
the business *do differently on Monday*? If the answer is "the same work, called something
else", it is not a territory. "Audit varian" renamed "Pengunci Varian" is a name change;
"install the master table, validate on upload, review weekly, report preventable errors" is a
territory. One model's own user caught this: *"A masih terasa audit yang diganti nama."*

The operational change still needs a buying reason. A better process does not prove that the
target segment will purchase a separately named expert method. Apply `market-adaptation.md`:
seller pages are supply, cross-segment evidence keeps its transfer gap, and an unvalidated
method is bundled into the outcome or tested—not installed as positioning.

**Reject a territory that violates the constraint register.** A position the user has said they
will not operate is not a candidate, however well it scores. Check `REFUSE` before scoring, not
after.

## Same-product test

When 98% of the product matches competitors, make the remaining 2% meaningful. Ask:

1. Which buying or use friction is specific and observable?
2. Which small product, packaging, service, or distribution change removes it?
3. Can that change be understood in five seconds?
4. Can the user deliver it consistently?
5. Does it create a reason to choose now rather than a clever slogan?

Example hypothesis: a reading product packaged like a work notebook may reduce social friction for commuters. This is not yet positioning. Research commuter behavior, the actual friction, religious/product norms, production cost, and whether buyers care before recommending it.

## Territory card

For each territory, produce:

```markdown

- Remember us when:
- Buyer and job:
- Alternative replaced:
- Evidence of tension:
- Promise:
- Reason to believe:
- What changes in product/service/packaging/channel:
- User's unfair familiarity or access:
- Why competitors cannot copy immediately:
- Who it is not for:
- Main unknown:
- Cheapest falsification test:
```

Territory names are working memory cues, not final taglines.

## Score before choosing

Score 0–2 and explain each score:

| Criterion | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Personal proof | no connection | plausible skill | artifact/result/access |
| Customer evidence | invented | weak signal | repeated credible signal |
| Distinctiveness | adjective | narrower niche | different buying/use logic |
| Feasibility | costly/unclear | possible | small operational change |
| Economics | likely negative | unknown | plausible with assumptions shown |
| Distribution access | no path | generic channel | reachable audience/partner |
| Defensibility | instantly copied | execution needed | proof/data/relationship/system compounds |

Do not average away a fatal zero in customer evidence, feasibility, or economics. Test or reject it.

## Position output

The chosen territory needs:

- **memory cue:** the moment/category buyers should associate with it;
- **promise:** the useful outcome in customer language;
- **reason to believe:** proof, mechanism, or credible constraint;
- **operating implications:** what product, packaging, service, pricing, channel, and content must change;
- **exclusions:** who/what the brand will not chase;
- **falsification:** what result would show the position is wrong.

A public line may follow, but it is the last step. Use the syntax that sounds natural for the category; do not force every brand into “Saya membantu…”.

## When to keep the ordinary position

Novelty is not a requirement. Keep a straightforward category position when research shows:

- buyers value availability, proof, price, or service more than a new frame;
- the clever concept adds explanation cost;
- operations cannot deliver the distinction;
- the audience is too small or inaccessible;
- a second brand would split scarce attention and budget.

Explain the evidence and choose an operational advantage instead of inventing a theme.
